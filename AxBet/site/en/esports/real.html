Games covered in the esports section

Each title plays by different rules, and that shapes what kind of bets make sense on it. Some run on tight round-based structures where a single map swings the whole handicap picture, while others stretch across a full best-of-five with room for momentum to shift twice or three times. Knowing which type a game falls into matters before picking a market, not after.

  • Dota 2 is a 5v5 MOBA, where group-stage matches run best-of-one before that shifts to best-of-three or best-of-five once knockout rounds start. The change in format changes how markets behave too.

  • CS2 is a round-based tactical FPS. Map handicaps and round totals carry noticeably more weight here than on titles without that structure, since every round adds up.

  • League of Legends sits in 5v5 MOBA territory, where a new patch can reshuffle the whole meta within days, and pick and draft markets feel that shift faster than the win line does.

  • Valorant is an agent-based tactical shooter, where whichever side wins the map vote often walks in favoured before the first round even starts, since the map itself carries an advantage.

  • Rainbow Six Siege runs a pre-map operator-ban phase, essentially a draft round bolted onto the match itself, on top of being a tactical FPS at its core.

  • StarCraft II — classic 1v1 real-time strategy. Best-of-five becomes standard once a bracket reaches its playoff stage, raising the stakes of any single game.

  • King of Glory is a mobile-first 5v5 MOBA. Matches typically wrap up faster than their PC counterparts do, which changes the pace of in-play betting.

  • Mobile Legends. Another 5v5 MOBA, whose competitive calendar barely slows down across the year, keeping fixtures listed almost continuously.

  • Warcraft III sits on a smaller circuit, covering 1v1 and 2v2 real-time strategy. What does run stays consistently listed on the platform.

A handful of smaller titles sit outside this main ten, since PUBG, Arena of Valor and Standoff 2 appear in the live discipline tabs with fewer daily fixtures, and coverage isn't equal across the board: CS2 usually carries the fullest schedule, sometimes topping a hundred events, with Dota 2 and League of Legends close behind, while Warcraft III mostly surfaces around its named events rather than running continuously.

Odds movement - what changes them

Odds on esports matches don't drift the way they sometimes do on slower sports, since a single in-game event can shift a line within seconds, and the reason behind that shift is almost always specific to the title being played, which is why the factors below are concrete triggers, not vague claims about form or momentum.

  • A Roshan kill or a first tower going down shifts Dota 2 and League of Legends lines within seconds, since the market reacts almost instantly to the objective, not just the kill count.

  • News of a substitute player stepping in shortly before a match tends to move the pre-match line more sharply than most other single factors, since roster strength is central to the pricing.

  • Once both sides confirm their map veto, the range of realistic outcomes narrows and the odds tighten to match, reflecting fewer possible paths to a result.

  • On CS2, dropping the pistol round often sets the tone for the whole half that follows, and the market prices that specific result in almost immediately.

  • A delayed stream means the number on screen can move a beat or two before the viewer sees why, though it's rarely a big jump, usually just a small correction.

These rarely move on their own, since a bad map veto paired with a last-minute roster change for the same side tends to push the line further than either factor alone, and reading just one signal in isolation can be misleading.
